In one sentence: products today are made in slices spread across many countries, a powerful "lead firm" coordinates those slices, and a country benefits only if it can plug into these chains and move up to higher-value work.

Basic Concepts
Definitions

Value chain, global value chain, and production network

A value chain is the full set of activities needed to bring a product from idea to customer and beyond: conception, design, sourcing, production, marketing, distribution, and after-sales support (Abonyi; Sturgeon). When these activities are spread across national borders, it becomes a global value chain (GVC).

A production network is the web of relationships that binds a group of firms into a larger economic unit (Sturgeon). The easiest way to remember the difference: a chain describes the sequence of activities, while a network describes the relationships between the firms doing them.

The global production network (GPN) approach studies the same thing but brings in the state, institutions, and places more directly. It uses ideas like territorial "embeddedness" and "strategic coupling," meaning how a region links its own assets to the needs of global lead firms (Neilson et al.).

An iPhone is designed in the US, uses chips from Taiwan and Korea, is assembled in China, and is sold worldwide. The list of steps is the value chain; the contracts and ties among Apple, its suppliers, and its assemblers form the production network.

Value chain vs supply chain

The two terms are often mixed up, and the readings define the difference in two ways:

  • Abonyi: a supply chain covers a firm's inbound and outbound logistics, while a value chain covers all activities, regardless of which firm does them.
  • Sturgeon: a supply chain is the value chain minus the lead firm's own activities.

Once we know what a chain is, the next question is who controls it. That is the job of the lead firm.

Lead Firms and Governance

Lead firm and governance

The lead firm develops and markets the final product and starts the flow of resources and information along the chain. Examples are Nike, Dell, Toyota, and Walmart. Governance is the lead firm's ability to coordinate and control many other firms "at a distance": who supplies, what, how much, when, and how far suppliers are allowed to upgrade.

Three dimensions and five types of actors

Sturgeon describes chains along three dimensions: organizational scale (value thread, supply thread, value chain, supply chain), spatial scale (local, domestic, international, regional, global; "global" means at least two continents or trade blocs), and productive actors.

The five actors are: (1) integrated firm, (2) retailer, (3) lead firm, (4) turn-key supplier, and (5) component supplier. A turn-key supplier provides a full package (sourcing, manufacturing, logistics) with little input from the lead firm, such as Celestica or Li & Fung.

Sturgeon warns about labels. "False homogeneity" means one word covers very different firms (calling both Ford and Dell an "OEM"). "False heterogeneity" means many words describe the same thing (contract manufacturer, full-package supplier, first-tier supplier).

Lead firms split production on purpose. The next topic explains the economics behind that choice.

Why Production Is Split Across Countries
Theory

Fragmentation theory and service link costs

Fragmentation theory (Jones and Kierzkowski, 1990, used by Kimura) says a firm breaks production into production blocks in different places, connected by service links. It only makes sense to do this if the savings in production cost are bigger than the extra service link costs.

Service link costs are the costs of connecting and coordinating activities across locations: transport, telecommunications, and coordination. They depend on trade and investment rules, logistics, property rights, and contract enforcement (Athukorala; Kimura).

A firm saves 30% in labor cost by assembling in Vietnam. If shipping, customs delays, and coordination add only 10%, fragmentation pays. If poor ports and slow customs add 35%, it does not.

First vs second unbundling (Baldwin): the first unbundling was an industry-by-industry division of labor between countries; the second is a task-by-task division inside production networks.

Global production sharing is the cross-border spreading of slices (tasks) of one production process. Athukorala calls the result a global manufacturing value chain (GMVC) to focus on trade and investment policy.

Supplemental: Ge et al. (2004)

The sourcing choices a firm faces

Crossing governance (insource or outsource) with location (domestic or offshore) gives four options: domestic insourcing, offshore insourcing (a "captive" unit abroad), domestic outsourcing, and offshore outsourcing. Hybrids (joint ventures) and recursive sourcing also exist.

The firm makes four decisions in order: WHICH processes to source, WHERE to locate them, WHOM to hire, and HOW to implement. Risks to watch: strategic vulnerability, incomplete contracts, hold-up, and the need for service level agreements.

Joining a chain is only the first step. The real goal for a developing country is to move into better-paying parts of the chain.

Upgrading and the Smiling Curve

Four types of upgrading (Abonyi)

  1. Process upgrading: doing the same work more efficiently.
  2. Product upgrading: making better or more sophisticated products.
  3. Functional upgrading: moving into new functions, such as from manufacturing into design.
  4. Chain or inter-sectoral upgrading: jumping to a new industry, as Taiwan did from microwaves to PCs.

The smiling curve shows why upgrading matters. Value is highest at the two ends (R&D and design on one side, marketing and services on the other) and lowest in the middle (plain production and assembly).

Abonyi also sees GVCs as a "second-best" route for SMEs. The chain is tiered (lead firm, then first-tier global suppliers, then lower-tier SMEs), and support services for SMEs are judged on outreach, sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and impact. Clusters such as Sialkot, Tirupur, and Penang show how SMEs join.

The Big Research Questions (Neilson et al., 2014)

Three issues the GVC/GPN field must face

  1. The role of the state. GVC research treats the state as background, while GPN research brings it in. State action and inaction (wages, tariffs, taxes, infrastructure, education, R&D, free trade zones) decide whether firms can join and upgrade. Korea acted as an intermediary enabling "strategic coupling" with global lead firms.
  2. Market shifts after the 2008 crisis. China became the "factory of the world" and India the "back office of the world." End markets shifted to Asia, and big Southern suppliers became partners rather than dependents.
  3. Theory lags behind evidence. Typologies of governance need causal explanations.

They also note two debates: "deep" critical analysis vs "shallow" technocratic problem-solving, and the politics of research (you cannot study a chain "from nowhere").

If the state matters, what should government policy look like in a world of GVCs? Gereffi and Sturgeon, and Kimura, answer this.

GVCs and Development Strategy
Gereffi and Sturgeon

Three types of industrial policy

  1. Horizontal (economy-wide): infrastructure, STEM education, workforce training, R&D, FDI rules, tax incentives.
  2. Selective or vertical (industry-specific): justified by strategic natural resources, strong forward and backward linkages, national security, or infant industries. This is the old import-substitution (ISI) logic.
  3. GVC-oriented: local-content rules to attract global suppliers, easier import of intermediate goods, using GVC links to upgrade domestic firms, and specializing in GVC niches.

How GVC-oriented policy differs from old ISI

  1. It targets global suppliers that serve many lead firms. Brazil courted Foxconn, not Apple.
  2. It accepts global sourcing and import dependence, and specializes in niches.
  3. It aims for local lead firms that use global suppliers located in the country.
GVC-oriented industrial policy is not a return to ISI. ISI tried to build whole national industries behind walls; GVC policy courts global suppliers and accepts imports.
Kimura

How East Asia used production networks

  • To join: a country needs only minimal location advantages (electricity, industrial estates, an investment agency, which can start inside special economic zones) plus low service link costs.
  • Early stage: attract production blocks, not whole industries. This differs from the ISI path of Japan, Korea, and Taiwan.
  • Middle-income stage: use industrial agglomeration, which (1) keeps "footloose" factories from leaving, (2) lets local suppliers join MNE networks, and (3) gives access to MNE technology. Then build human capital, R&D, and city infrastructure. Bangkok has 40+ industrial estates within 100 km.
  • Policy matrix: cut network set-up costs, service link costs, and production costs, across geographic distance and agglomeration. This underlies the ASEAN Economic Community and RCEP.
  • Shocks: networks pass on shocks (2008 crisis, 2011 Japan earthquake, Thai floods) but also stabilize economies, because firms work hard to keep their links alive.
A shock spreading through production networks is not the same as financial contagion. Firms fight to restore their links.

With these ideas, we can now read the Philippine case and see why the country did not move up as fast as its neighbors.

The Philippine Story (Athukorala, 2021)
Philippine case

A tale of "arrested growth," in order

  1. Promising start (early 1970s): US ties (Laurel-Langley Agreement), good location, educated English-speaking workers. The Bataan EPZ opened (established 1969, operating 1973). Intel arrived in 1974, followed by Texas Instruments, Motorola, and Philips.
  2. Lost decade (1980s): Laurel-Langley ended in 1974; cronyism, protectionism, and instability (People Power 1986, coup attempts, the 1989 kidnapping of Japanese executives). The country missed three investment waves: the first hard-disk-drive wave, Japanese FDI after the Plaza Accord, and apparel relocation.
  3. Ramos era (1992 to 1998): stability, liberalization, and the Foreign Investment Act of 1991 brought a Japanese hard-disk boom (Hitachi, Fujitsu, Toshiba, NEC).
  4. After 1998: FDI outside EPZs was still capped at minority stakes; growth leaned on remittances and BPO; Intel left for Vietnam in 2008. Autos were a bright spot (Toyota wire harnesses and transmissions).

Athukorala's main argument

The problem was not GVCs themselves. It was a dualistic incentive structure: export manufacturing was kept inside enclave EPZs with special incentives, while the rest of the economy stayed restricted. Growth was "arrested" inside the zones.

Volume factor: a low domestic value-added ratio is normal in production sharing. A country gains by expanding total output, not by insisting that each unit have high local content.

Earning 10% local value on $10 billion of exports ($1 billion) beats earning 40% on $1 billion of exports ($400 million). Volume matters.

Three things that decide whether a country joins production networks

  1. Wages of trainable workers.
  2. Political stability and policy certainty.
  3. Country-specific service link costs.

Numbers worth remembering

  • GMVC products are about 90% of manufacturing exports (2015 to 2019); producer-driven chains are 94% of these; semiconductors are over 40%.
  • Manufacturing fell from over 25% to about 17% of GDP.
  • Doing Business rank 95; electricity at 18.10 US cents per kWh, second-highest in Asia.

The readings then move from the past to new pressures. COVID-19 exposed how fragile long chains can be.

COVID-19 and Resilient Chains
Sydow et al. (2021)

Why Germany coped well, and what resilience means

Germany handled the first wave well because of: a well-equipped, decentralized public health system; a strong state budget (the "schwarze Null," or balanced budget) that allowed large stimulus packages; and crisis-tested tools, especially Kurzarbeit (short-time work, where government pays up to 87% of lost income).

Resilience is a network's ability to keep delivering under shocks. Suggested measures: slack resources and redundancy, back-shoring critical items, new inventory strategies, moving from single to dual sourcing, avoiding over-concentration of supply, an internal second supplier, data analytics to spot bottlenecks, and looser coupling with closer collaboration (unions, government, NGOs).

De-globalization is unlikely. The aim is "intelligent coupling rather than decoupling."
Phillips et al. (2022)

Redistributed manufacturing and four GVC models

Redistributed manufacturing (RDM) is small-scale, local, flexible production close to where it is needed, made possible by 3D printing and microfactories.

During COVID-19, local makers 3D-printed face shields and ventilator parts near hospitals instead of waiting for shipments from abroad.

Three drivers of reconfiguration seen in COVID-19

  1. Delivery assurance
  2. Procurement capabilities

The Digital Economy (Cho et al., 2018)

Digital segments, trends, and Korea's weaknesses

Three digital segments, resting on telecom and internet infrastructure: (1) software (application software including SaaS, and systems software); (2) IT services (consulting, BPO, analytics); (3) internet software and services platforms (search, social, cloud, e-commerce).

Servitization means manufacturers sell services bundled with products (maintenance, "as-a-service"). Servicification means manufacturing uses more services inside its own process.

Seven trends

  1. Services redistribute value (IBM hardware fell from 46% to 8% of revenue).
  2. Data lets equipment makers upgrade functionally into consulting.
  3. Advertising separates the product from the revenue.
  4. New skills are needed.
  5. Certifications act as education.
  6. Collaborative ecosystems.
  7. US dominance (67% of the top 100 digital multinationals).

Korea's five weaknesses

Limited global participation; firms captive to chaebol parents; R&D kept at home and misaligned (services only 8% of business R&D vs OECD 38%); few acquisitions and little venture capital; few international partnerships. The recommendation is a proactive international approach: foreign incubators, VC, partnerships, certifications, and investment promotion.

Part A. Channels and Mechanisms of International Technology Transfer
What Technology Transfer Is
Definitions

Technology and technology transfer

Technology is "the way we do things." More formally, it combines hard elements (equipment) and soft elements (knowledge, skills, organization) used to reach an objective (Rani).

Technology transfer (TT) is the process of moving scientific findings, knowledge, processes, and technologies from a source (owner) to a receiver (beneficiary) for further development and commercialization (Rani). UNCTAD's definition (via Radosevic) adds that it is the transfer of systematic knowledge for making a product, applying a process, or rendering a service, and is not the mere sale or lease of goods.

Buying 100 laptops is a sale of goods. Getting the design files, training, and process know-how so your engineers can build the laptops is technology transfer.

Tacit vs codified knowledge, and absorptive capacity

Codified knowledge is written down: blueprints, patents, manuals. Tacit knowledge lives in people and routines and is hard to write down. Kogut and Zander: tacit, complex technology tends to move through a wholly-owned subsidiary; codifiable technology can move through licensing.

Absorptive capacity (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990) is the receiver's ability to recognize, absorb, and apply outside knowledge. It is a key success factor and will appear again in Modules 5, 7, and 8.

Radosevic separates technology as information (generic, cheap to copy) from technology as knowledge (firm-specific, tacit, "localized"). So trade in technology transfers the elements, but not the capabilities. The Soviet Union imported machines but never the capability, so its technology "froze."

Technology has a life of its own inside a firm. Rani's life cycle shows the stages a firm manages, starting from awareness.

Life Cycle and Types of Transfer (Rani et al.)

Technology life cycle: five phases

  1. Awareness: a formal way of spotting emerging technologies.
  2. Acquisition: technical and economic feasibility studies, compared with developing it in-house.
  3. Adaptation: tailoring the technology to local needs (planned before acquiring).
  4. Advancement: improving it over time from innovation and market feedback.
  5. Abandonment: deciding when it is obsolete. Rani calls this the most critical phase because there is no easy formula.

Five classifications of technology transfer

  1. International: across borders.
  2. Regional: between states or regions within a country.
  3. Cross-industry or cross-sector: e.g., from the space program to commercial products.
  4. Inter-firm: e.g., CAD/CAM moving from a machine-tool firm to a furniture firm.
  5. Intra-firm: from one division or department to another.

Relocation/absorption paradigm: TT has two general stages, (1) technology relocation and (2) technology absorption, with human (conceptual) knowledge moving alongside hard technology.

Five stages of TT from rich to developing countries (Khalil and Shankar)

  1. Sale and licensing of industrial property (patents, utility models, designs, trademarks).
  2. Know-how and technical expertise (feasibility studies, plans, specifications, advisers, training).
  3. Basic or detailed engineering design, installation, and operation of the plant.
  4. Lease or purchase of machinery, equipment, intermediates, and raw materials tied to TT.
  5. Industrial and technical cooperation agreements (turnkey, international subcontracting, management and marketing services).

Now the core of 2-A: the actual routes technology takes. Both readings sort them into planned and unplanned channels.

Channels of Technology Transfer
Rani et al.

Three channels of technology flow

  • General channels: unintentional, with no continuing role for the source. Education, training, publications, conferences, study missions, visits.
  • Reverse engineering: the fastest way to copy a technology without a formal transfer (Module 5-B covers this in depth).
  • Formal (planned) channels: intentional, with the owner's consent:
    • Licensing (lump sum and/or a percentage of sales)
    • Franchise (licensing plus continuing support, e.g., McDonald's)
    • Joint venture
    • Turnkey project (a complete plant handed over ready to run)
    • FDI (technology stays inside the firm)
    • Technical consortium or joint R&D
Rani's "general channels" are unintentional. "Formal channels" are planned and have the owner's consent.
Wahab et al.

Formal (market) vs informal (non-market) channels

Formal or market channels involve an explicit transaction: exporting, FDI, licensing, joint ventures. Informal or non-market channels have no formal transaction and no payment to the owner.

Another split: internalized transfer happens inside the MNC (FDI into a wholly-owned subsidiary), while externalized transfer crosses firm boundaries (exporting, licensing, JV).

Millman's six motives for licensing (in order)

  1. Fill gaps in the product range.
  2. Let a small firm commercialize fast in a big market.
  3. Diversify.
  4. Earn a return on technology outside the licensor's strategy.
  5. Exploit a process or quality breakthrough.
  6. Separate marketing from manufacturing.

What makes an international JV succeed

JV traits (organizational distance, openness, relationship quality) and partner traits (protectiveness, motivation, prior experience, absorptive capacity, learning intent).

Five non-market channels

  1. Imitation: inspection, reverse engineering, software decompilation, with no compensation.
  2. Movement of personnel: expatriates, expert visits, ex-employees joining rivals or starting firms.
  3. Data in patent applications and test data.
  4. Communication media: documents, journals, spin-offs, cooperative R&D.
  5. Temporary migration: students and scientists studying or working abroad.
A Filipino engineer works five years at a Japanese electronics plant in Laguna, then starts a local parts supplier. The know-how moved through "movement of personnel," with no license and no payment to the Japanese firm.

Technology spillovers

Spillovers are benefits that leak to local firms because the MNC cannot capture the full value of its technology. They travel through three routes: demonstration and imitation, labor turnover, and backward and forward linkages (local firms supplying or buying from the MNC).

Does the channel really matter? Three positions

  1. Yes: the 1960s to 1970s view ranked channels by cost (JV over FDI, licensing over JV, direct purchase over licensing) and preferred "unbundling" the package, though that can raise learning costs.
  2. Secondary: how the transfer is implemented and how capability is built matter more than the channel.
  3. Depends on the industry: FDI dominates differentiated, high-skill industries; licensing dominates less complex, capital-goods-intensive ones.

Other points: TT costs averaged 19% of project cost (Teece). The Philippines had a chip-assembly boom but stalled for lack of a capital-goods sector. Radosevic also describes the OEM → ODM → OBM path: from making goods for a buyer's brand, to designing them, to selling your own brand.

Issues, Challenges, and Policy
Rani et al.

Issues in technology transfer (in order)

  1. Technology acquisition as a make-or-buy decision: internal (exclusive ownership, needs capability), external (less cost, time, and risk, but needs adaptation), or combined.
  2. Choice of technology: it must be appropriate, matched to local resources and skills. Rich-country technology is often too capital-intensive.
  3. Terms and conditions: watch for restrictive clauses, such as bans on local R&D or requiring local improvements to be handed back. About 90% of modern TT to developing countries is controlled by MNCs.
  4. Creating local capability.
  5. Globalization under WTO rules.
  6. Thirteen barriers, e.g., no framework, no systematic planning, no mutual benefit, ignoring ergonomics and environment, feasibility limited to financial analysis, ignoring end-of-life disposal.
  7. HR issues: culture, skills, language, attitude.

What host governments can do

Absorb technology; set import policies; reserve sectors for domestic ownership; cap inward investment; impose performance requirements; discourage restrictive clauses; influence terms; fund local R&D; promote cross-border collaborative R&D; and encourage local production after TT.

Rani's conclusion: TT is continuous, not a one-time handover. Without support and learning, transferred technology becomes obsolete.
Supplemental: Barton (2007), Foray (2009)

Barton: the 1970s debate was about hidden costs, restrictive clauses, and second-rate technology. Today developing countries are more capable and production is global, but TRIPS and free-trade rules make old routes (imitation, protected home markets) harder. TT is basically a flow of knowledge between people. Subsidize only when social benefits clearly exceed private returns.

Foray: for least-developed countries, FDI and licenses alone deliver too little TT. Developed countries should fund TT as a main project, led by local actors through public-private partnerships, to honor TRIPS Article 66.2 (incentives to transfer technology to LDCs).

Part B. The Nature, Benefits, and Costs of Foreign Direct Investment

Part A showed that FDI is the main channel for new, complex technology. Part B asks what FDI is, why firms do it, and whether host countries actually gain.

What FDI Is
Definitions

FDI vs portfolio investment

FDI is investment that gives a foreign investor a lasting interest in and control of an enterprise in another economy. The IMF and World Bank threshold is 10% or more of voting stock. FDI is the sum of equity capital, reinvested earnings, and other long- and short-term capital (Alfaro).

Portfolio investment is buying foreign securities without control. The wish for control is what separates FDI from portfolio investment.

A Japanese firm buying 40% of a Cebu shipyard and sitting on its board is FDI. A Japanese pension fund buying 0.5% of a listed Philippine bank is portfolio investment.
  • MNE controls and manages plants in at least two countries; MNC/TNC owns and controls income-generating assets in more than one country.
  • Greenfield FDI builds new facilities from scratch; brownfield (M&A) buys an existing firm.
  • Horizontal FDI repeats the same stage abroad (often to serve that market); vertical FDI splits the value chain by function to cut costs; export-platform FDI sells the affiliate's output to third countries.
Theory

Why firms invest abroad

Four motives (Dunning and Lundan, 2008): natural-resource-seeking, market-seeking, efficiency-seeking, and strategic-asset-seeking.

OLI or eclectic paradigm (Dunning): a firm invests abroad when it has all three advantages:

  • Ownership: a firm-specific asset, such as a technology or brand.
  • Location: the host offers cheap inputs, resources, or a way around tariffs.
  • Internalization: it is better to keep the asset inside the firm than to license it out.

OLI builds on Hymer (1960): some assets are worth more under foreign control. The simple cost-of-capital theory fails to explain FDI.

Internalization is the part of OLI that explains why a firm chooses FDI over licensing.

Countries chase FDI because of what it might bring local firms. The evidence on these "spillovers" is the heart of 2-B.

Benefits: Spillovers and Linkages
Alfaro (2014)

What the evidence says

  • MNEs are more productive than local firms.
  • Horizontal spillovers (to firms in the same industry) are near zero on average.
  • Vertical or backward spillovers (to local suppliers) are positive (Javorcik 2004 for Lithuania; Blalock and Gertler for Indonesia).
  • Market-stealing effect (Aitken and Harrison 1999, Venezuela): FDI raises productivity in the plants it enters but lowers it in domestic plants by taking their market share, so the net effect is small.

Complementarities: local conditions that must be present

  • Outward-oriented trade policy.
  • A minimum level of human capital (Borensztein et al. 1998).
  • Developed financial markets (Alfaro et al. 2004, working through productivity).
  • Good institutions (the "Lucas paradox," that capital does not flow to poor countries, is mostly explained by institutional quality).

Not all FDI is equal

Greenfield helps growth while M&A is neutral or negative in developing countries; investor origin matters; manufacturing FDI is more positive than services; primary-sector FDI tends to be negative. Effects on competitors dip then rise; effects on suppliers are temporary.

Motivating case: Intel's 1996 Costa Rica plant was six times the country's yearly FDI. Assembly moved to Asia in 2014, but R&D and services stayed. Central America's FDI came in cycles: agricultural and mining enclaves, tariff-jumping under import substitution, then export-led EPZs, then services and high-tech.

World Bank GIC Ch. 2 (Reyes)

FDI and high-growth firms

FDI's net effect = positive knowledge transfer minus negative competition. High-growth firms are domestic firms in the top 5% of job growth over two years. They are small and young and more common in services.

Two channels: the linkages channel (local firms become suppliers to foreign firms) and the demonstration channel (local firms copy by watching or hiring ex-MNC workers).

  • The average domestic firm gains nothing significant (competition outweighs benefits; low absorptive capacity).
  • High-growth firms do gain, mainly through linkages: 1 percentage point more local sourcing by foreign firms gives +0.6 units of output growth; demonstration only +0.1.
  • In manufacturing only linkages matter; in services both do. Sub-Saharan Africa shows no spillovers (resource FDI).

Linkage programs should target high-potential firms at three levels: market (supplier databases, matchmaking), supply (supplier development, certification, clusters), and demand (incentives for local sourcing). Compulsory local-content rules discourage FDI.

Chile's Supplier Development Program (CORFO, 1998) had a diagnostic stage (up to 6 months) and a development stage (up to 3 years), with CORFO paying up to 50%.

If spillovers depend on conditions, governments must decide how to attract the right investors. The World Bank survey shows what investors actually care about.

What Investors Want (World Bank GIC Ch. 1)

Top five findings

  2. More than a third reinvest all profits in the host country, so expansion-friendly policies matter.
  3. Investment protection guarantees are critical: over 90% rate legal protections as important, and 45% call them deal-breakers, the highest of any factor.

COVID-19 and FDI (Hayakawa et al., 2022)

Global FDI fell 35% in 2020 (from US$1.5 trillion to US$1 trillion), more than GDP (-3.2%) or trade (-8.3%). The study used quarterly data for 173 home and 192 host countries, measuring severity by cases, deaths, and the Oxford stringency index.

  • Manufacturing: damage in the host country cuts both greenfield and M&A (10% more host cases, about 1.4% less greenfield). Damage in the home country is not negative, and even positive for greenfield, as firms shift production platforms abroad.
  • Services: host and home damage cut greenfield; M&A is unaffected in the same quarter.
  • One quarter later: host damage cuts M&A in both sectors (signed contracts delay withdrawal).
  • OECD investors kept doing M&A regardless. Announced deals react faster than completed ones.

Fire-sale FDI is buying local firms cheaply when a crisis lowers their value.

Greenfield is hit right away (lockdowns stop construction); M&A is hit with a lag.
Costs of FDI and Incentive Policy

The downside

FDI can crowd out local firms from credit, act as an enclave with few local ties, steal market share, and create hidden costs. Investment incentives (fiscal, financial, or other, such as zones and infrastructure) are justified only by a market failure such as spillovers. Otherwise countries risk a race to the bottom, outbidding each other until incentives exceed the gains. SEZs can become "safety valves rather than catalysts" for reform. The WTO barred export-contingent subsidies.

Alfaro's conclusion: FDI helps most through suppliers. Instead of incentives, remove barriers to linkages and improve local firms' access to inputs, technology, and finance.

Supplemental: Gürak (2003)

Hidden costs of technology transfer through FDI

Transfer pricing is when an MNC sets prices between its own units (overpricing imports to the subsidiary, underpricing its exports) to move profits out of the host country.

Hoffmann-La Roche's UK subsidiary declared about 5% profit while its true return was over 70% (1966 to 1972).

  8. Technological dependence

MNCs package technology for three reasons: control, quality and trademark protection, and keeping out local competitors.

Part A. Anatomy of Technology Licensing Agreements
What a License Is
Definitions

License, licensor, licensee

A license agreement is a contract that lets one party use something owned or controlled by another, usually in exchange for payment or other value (Leute). Without it, using the IP is infringement (EU IPR Helpdesk). WIPO puts it simply: "consent by the owner to use IP in exchange for money or something else of value." The licensor grants the rights; the licensee receives them.

A license is permission, not a sale. Ownership stays with the licensor and the two sides enter a long-term relationship. An assignment, in contrast, transfers ownership in a one-time transaction.

A university that licenses its patented water filter to a Philippine manufacturer still owns the patent. If it assigns the patent, the manufacturer becomes the new owner.

A patent is a "negative right"

A patent lets the owner stop others from using the invention; it does not by itself give anyone the right to do something. So a non-exclusive patent license is basically a promise not to sue ("permission to infringe" in Leute; "covenant not to sue" in Smith).

WIPO (2015)

Six fundamental ideas about licensing

  1. Licensing happens only when one party owns valuable IP and can legally exclude the other. No IP, no licensing.
  2. There are three categories of licenses: for certain IP rights; for all IP needed to make a technology's products; and for all IP needed to comply with a technical standard (patent pools, RAND terms).
  3. Licensing sits inside a business relationship with related agreements (R&D, manufacturing, sales).
  4. Interests differ but must meet. A deal is about more than "how much?"; aim for win-win.
  5. Many complex terms mean advance preparation is essential.
  6. Licensing is not the same as technology transfer. Transfer happens only when the licensee learns to use, adapt, and improve the technology.

WIPO also lists three ways to exploit IP: defensive, offensive, and as leverage in deals (JV, R&D, M&A).

EU IPR Helpdesk

Why license, and what can go wrong

The choice of how to commercialize IP depends on (1) business objectives, (2) the form of IP, and (3) economic resources. IP risk = likelihood × consequences.

BenefitsRisks

Compared with alternatives: a license does not transfer ownership (unlike assignment); it lets an existing organization exploit IP without forming a new company (unlike a spin-off); and it grants user rights rather than only the right to resell (unlike a distribution agreement).

The most important choice in any license is how exclusive it is. Everything else, including price, depends on it.

"Sole" is the usual trick. Under a sole license the licensor grants no other licenses but keeps its own right to use the IP.

Defining the scope

  • Field of use: the specific application covered, e.g., "treatment of eczema in humans." The licensor can license other fields to others.
  • Licensed territory: the geography (worldwide, EU, one country). Online sales need special thought.
  • Licensed patent: applications, foreign counterparts, divisionals, continuations, reissues; often excludes continuations-in-part (CIPs).
  • Licensed product: a product that would infringe without the license, or that uses the licensed technology.
  • Retained rights: rights the licensor keeps, such as freedom for academic research.
A university licenses its compound exclusively to Company A for "Alzheimer's therapeutics in Asia" and non-exclusively to Company B for "veterinary use worldwide." Field and territory let one invention earn from several licensees.

A license is a relational document: one clause affects the others. Write in plain language. "If you are not sure if something is clear, it likely isn't."

Diligence, warranties, and indemnities

Diligence clauses keep the technology from "collecting dust on a shelf." Vague "best efforts" promises should be backed by measurable milestones and minimum payments.

A warranty is a written guarantee (e.g., of title or validity); universities rarely give them. An indemnity protects against loss; the licensee usually indemnifies the institution except for gross negligence or willful misconduct.

Infringement, termination, and sublicensing

Suspected infringement (exclusive license sample clauses): (1) the licensor has the first right to sue, and the licensee has 15 days to join; (2) a joint suit splits costs and recovery equally; (3) the licensee may sue if the licensor does not, bearing all costs; (4) recovery for past sales is treated as net sales; (5) a party abandoning the suit gives notice so the other may continue. Under a non-exclusive license, the licensee informs and the licensor decides.

Licensor's grounds to terminate: late payments or reports, missed diligence, other material breach, false reports, always with notice and a cure period. The licensee can usually terminate any time on notice.

Sublicensing: how many layers are allowed; which provisions flow down (warranties, indemnities, reports, audits); a copy of the sublicense; the licensor's share of sublicense income; what happens on termination; and required sublicensing for neglected markets (e.g., the developing world).

US context

Bayh-Dole Act (35 USC 200 to 204): government rights

  1. Nonprofits and small businesses may own federally funded inventions if they comply.
  2. They must disclose inventions to the government within a reasonable time.
  3. They must elect to retain title within a set period.
  4. The government gets a non-exclusive right to use the invention for its own purposes.
  5. They must notify the government if they will not pursue the invention.
  6. Nonprofits may not assign without approval, must share royalties with inventors, use the rest for research and education, and prefer small businesses.
  7. March-in rights for the government.
  8. Exclusive licensees must make products for US sale "substantially in the United States" unless waived.

Combination products, stacking, and MFN

Combination products clause: royalty base = net sales × (market value of licensed product ÷ total market value of all components).

Royalty stacking is when several royalties pile up on one product because several licenses are needed (totals of about 6% to 20% of sales, per Jones et al.). Leute's sample clause: the licensee may credit 50% of third-party royalties, but the royalty is never reduced by more than 50%. Royalty packing is bundling many patents to justify a higher rate.

Golden Rice needed about 45 patents owned by 20+ parties. Stacking remedies include royalty ceilings (e.g., total capped at 6%, shared pro rata), floors (e.g., 5% may drop but not below 2%), caps on reductions, lump sums, and patent pools (Jones, Whitham and Handler).

Most Favored Nation (MFN) clause: if the licensor later gives a third party better terms, this licensee may claim them, but must accept the same burdens ("all terms or nothing"). It is hard to track and compare.

Cap vs minimum: a cap limits total payments (the licensee likes it); a minimum guarantees a floor (the licensor likes it, especially with exclusivity).

WIPO (2015)

How much? Three valuation methods

  1. Cost method: based on what the licensor spent. Self-serving and may have nothing to do with value. WIPO's example: a company that spent a fortune developing a smokeless cigarette nobody wanted.
  2. Income method: a share of expected earnings; use NPV for lump sums.
  3. Comparables or market method: royalty databases; true comparables are rare.

Rules of thumb: the 25% to 33% rule (licensor gets a quarter to a third of the licensee's benefit), which WIPO calls "almost useless"; most royalties fall between 0.5% and 5% of revenues. Watch for cannibalization of the licensor's own sales.

Royalties on an expired patent are never acceptable (Brulotte v. Thys). Collecting later for use during the patent term is fine.

Knowing the clauses is half the job. WIPO's main message is that good licenses come from preparation before anyone sits down to negotiate.

  3. Time frame.
  4. Data and documents.

Term sheet rules

Term sheet, first line, and bottom line

A term sheet is a short outline (2 pages or less) of the key business terms. There are two versions: internal (your positions and fallbacks, for your team only) and external (given to the other side). It is not binding, not a letter of intent, and never signed.

The first line is your opening ideal position, written in the external term sheet. The bottom line is the minimum you must get; it stays confidential and is not even written in the internal term sheet.

WIPO's internal term sheet covers 14 items: subject matter, ownership, related agreements, development, scope, improvements, sublicensing, territory, field of use, financial terms, term, future versions, obligations, disputes.

Key terms in four clusters (WIPO)

  1. Subject of the license: what exactly is licensed (licensor wants it narrow, licensee broad); is it finished (avoid vague "best efforts," use a separate development agreement); who owns it (beware joint ownership); can you see it first (confidentiality agreement); is a trademark license needed.
  2. Rights: scope (reproduce, modify, make, sell, import, sublicense, grant-back, "have made"); territory; exclusivity. Exclusivity is justified by investments that cannot be redeployed; limit it with minimums, a shorter head-start period, or partial (field) exclusivity. Non-competes are often illegal.
  3. Financial: how much; how to pay (royalties vs lump sums, advances, caps vs minimums, adjusted royalties such as 2% falling to 0.5%); cross-licenses; warranties and indemnities as financial risk; related financing or supply.
  4. Growth over time: future versions (avoid "agreements to agree"); service, support, spare parts; documentation, know-how, training; future relationship (avoid non-competes; consider non-solicitation).

Conducting the negotiation (WIPO)

  • Use the advantage continuum: score each term from +5 (most advantageous) to -5 (most disadvantageous) and stay on the positive side.
  • Prepare fallbacks and compromises. Failure can be success: walk away when bottom lines conflict.
  • Negotiating "style" is a myth. Success comes from (1) knowing your positions and fallbacks, (2) explaining your needs convincingly, and (3) confidence from preparation.
  • Start with a preliminary meeting and an external term sheet, not a contract draft. Write down progress but do not sign the term sheet. An unsigned agreement is not an agreement.
  • Model dispute clause: WIPO mediation, then expedited arbitration if unresolved in 60 days.
  • After signing, do not lock the agreement away: inform staff, track dates and royalties, assign responsibility.
WIPO/ITC (2005) suggests "if... then" offers: "If you accept a minimum of $50,000 rising to $75,000 and $100,000, then we can lower the royalty from 10% to 7.5% after the first volume tier."
Competition Law Limits

Licensing is generally pro-competitive. Being dominant is not illegal; abusing dominance is. Agreements between competitors (horizontal) get more scrutiny than vertical ones.

  • Per se illegal: banned without analysis (e.g., price fixing). In some places, no-challenge clauses and exclusive royalty-free grant-backs.
  • Rule of reason: weigh pro- and anti-competitive effects.
  • Tying / coercive package license: forcing a licensee to take unwanted technology or products.
  • Grant-back: licensee must give its improvements back. Mirror rights mean each side gives the other rights to future improvements.
  • Compulsory licensing: a license ordered by the state (TRIPS exceptions for public interest, non-working, emergencies).

WIPO's eleven restrictions to examine (none automatically illegal)

  1. Exclusive license in a field smaller than the licensee's usual business
  2. Exclusive cross-license
  3. Reciprocal rights restricting licensing to third parties
  4. Licensee must assign or exclusively license back all improvements
  5. Forced license of multiple IP rights, some unneeded
  6. Forced purchase of other products or services
  7. Licensor pays the licensee besides granting the license
  8. Licensee agrees not to compete
  9. Agreement not to deal with certain third parties
  10. Royalties on products unrelated to the licensed IP
  11. Obligations that last beyond the life of the IP

Part B. Anatomy of OEM and Subcontracting Agreements

Licensing moves rights to use technology. OEM and subcontracting move the actual manufacturing work. The first reading asks who should order parts and when; the rest show how the deal is written.

The Players
Definitions

OEM (original equipment manufacturer): in Wang et al., the brand owner that sells the finished product but outsources assembly. Contract manufacturer (CM): a factory that builds products for someone else's brand, e.g., Foxconn, Flextronics, Compal, Wistron. CMs gain from low wages, scale, and exposure to many OEMs' engineering. The supplier makes the components.

Who Orders and When (Wang et al., 2014)
Two managerial questions

Control vs delegation, push vs pull

QuestionOption 1Option 2
"Who should order?"Control: the OEM buys components itself, contracting separately with the CM and the supplier. CM and supplier set prices at the same time.Delegation: the OEM contracts only with the CM, which subcontracts the supplier. Pricing is sequential (supplier first, then CM).
"When to order?"Push: orders placed before demand is known. The OEM carries all inventory risk.Pull: orders placed after demand is known. The CM and supplier carry inventory and capacity risk.
Push: US fireworks retailers sell about 95% of their stock between 15 May and 4 July, and shipping from China takes 4 to 6 weeks, so they must order before they know demand. Pull: vendor-managed inventory, as Flextronics did for Lenovo, where the supplier keeps stock ready and ships as demand appears.

A two-wholesale-price (TWP) contract mixes both: pre-book at a lower price plus last-minute orders at a higher price (e.g., LCD panel advance commitments). The OEM's push decision is a classic newsvendor problem (one-period ordering under uncertain demand).

Key results

Double marginalization: each middleman adds its own markup, so the final price climbs. Under push + delegation, the CM's middleman role lets the supplier charge more, which raises the OEM's total price, so the OEM orders less, which hurts the CM too.

Markup pricing rule: under pull, the CM's and supplier's wholesale prices are proportional to their production costs.

  • The OEM always prefers control, and it prefers pull to push; production quantity is higher under pull.
  • With exogenous prices under push, the OEM prefers delegation only if delegation gives a lower total procurement price.
  • A TWP contract ends up behaving like pull when prices are endogenous, and the OEM still prefers control.
  • Preferences never line up for all three parties, only for two at a time.

Once the structure is chosen, the relationship is put on paper. The ITC model contract is the main template.

The Contract Manufacture Agreement (ITC, 2010)
Definition

In a contract manufacture agreement, a manufacturer designs, makes, and delivers goods that the client will build into its own products or services. The client relies on the manufacturer as the "most specialized party." The CISG (UN sales convention) is expressly excluded because it does not apply where labor or services are the main obligation (CISG Art. 3(2)).

The ITC book has nine model contracts (contractual alliance, corporate JV, sale of goods short and standard, long-term supply, contract manufacture, distribution, commercial agency, supply of services). They share harmonized boilerplate, are written simply for non-specialist SMEs, and incorporate nothing "by reference." The contract manufacture model is a "menu": a basic scheme plus options for client technology transfer (Art. 1.4), client equipment (Art. 1.5), and pre-production samples (Art. 1.6).

Articles of the model agreement (in order)

  1. Manufacture and supply: goods per Schedule 1; delivery terms; client technology kept confidential and used only for the client; client equipment stays the client's property but at the manufacturer's risk; no production until samples are approved in writing.
  2. Price: invoice after delivery, pay within 30 days.
  3. Late payment interest.
  4. Quality: manufacturer warrants conformity and freedom from defects for a set period (fix within 30 days of notice). The manufacturer indemnifies the client for product claims, except defects caused by the client's specs, technology, or equipment. No liability for lost profit; total liability capped at the price of the goods.
  5. IP and trademarks: the manufacturer uses client technology and marks only for the contract. The client indemnifies the manufacturer against third-party IP claims, except the manufacturer's own default.
  6. Cooperation for improvements: quarterly reviews. Each party owns its own improvements, but the client gets a non-exclusive, royalty-free, unlimited-time license to the manufacturer's improvements.
  7. Duration and termination: for uncured material breach (30 days), insolvency, ceasing business; client may terminate if samples fail.
  8. Consequences of termination: unsold goods offered at cost; stop using client IP; return documents and equipment.
  9. Confidentiality: no time limit.
  10. Force majeure: time extended; termination if it lasts more than 3 months.
  11. Hardship: still bound to perform, but may request renegotiation if events were unforeseeable and beyond control.
  12. No partnership or agency; no assignment or subcontracting without written approval; notices; entire agreement; severability; authorizations; dispute resolution (institutional arbitration); applicable law (CISG excluded).
Force majeure excuses delay from events beyond control (war, fire, flood). Hardship covers events that fundamentally change the deal's balance and lets a party ask to renegotiate.

Subcontracting
Wrigleys (2009)

What a subcontract is

A subcontract is when A hires B to do some or all of the work A promised to a main contractor. It is a separate contract; the main contract stays in force; A remains liable to the main contractor for B's defaults; and the main contractor usually cannot sue B, since they have no direct contract.

ArrangementWhat happens
SubcontractB performs; A stays fully liable
NovationB steps into A's shoes and A drops out
AssignmentA transfers the benefit of the main contract to B

Back-to-back: the subcontract mirrors the main contract so B's duties to A match A's duties to the main contractor. Simply saying the subcontract is "based on" the main contract is not enough.

A Manila IT firm wins a government help-desk contract (main contract) and hires a Cebu firm (B) to run the night shift. If the night shift fails, the agency goes after the Manila firm, which then claims against the Cebu firm under their back-to-back subcontract.

Practical guidance and common clauses

  • Check if the main contract allows subcontracting; get consent if needed.
  • Decide what information B needs, and protect A's sensitive data (e.g., pricing).
  • Under English law, writing is not required, so beware letters, emails, and letters of intent that create unintended contracts.
  • A may try to make terms tighter than the main contract (absolute duties vs reasonable endeavors; 14-day vs 30-day payment).
  • Common clauses: term no longer than the main contract; same service standard; payment ("pay when paid," which B resists); liability caps (B wants one); indemnities; termination rights for both sides; employee transfer rules (TUPE); sub-subcontracting; audits; variations; no partnership; third-party rights.

Reid Subcontracting Agreement (Australia): definitions (the main contract is the "Consultancy Agreement"; 12-month restraint period); subcontractor complies with the company's main-contract duties; fees paid within 30 days; nominated personnel replaced within 7 days; all IP vests in the company; warranties (no defects, no viruses); no-fault events allow termination after 60 days; termination on 1 month's notice or unremedied breach in 21 days; restraint / non-solicitation of staff and clients for 12 months.

Part A. Anatomy of Joint Venture Agreements
What a Joint Venture Is
Definitions

A joint venture (JV) is when two or more firms combine resources to create an independent but jointly owned entity with a specific purpose, sharing risks and profits (Beamish and Lupton, in Calle). Sharing equity raises commitment, but also moral hazard.

The PCC defines it as a business arrangement where two or more entities contribute capital, services, assets, or a combination to an investment or project, each with the right to direct and govern policies, intending to share profits, risks, and losses.

Incorporated (equity / corporate) JVContractual (unincorporated) JV
A new separate company is formed (the JV Company or JVC)Partners sign a binding agreement and perform functions without forming a company
Two firms set up "SolarPH Inc." owned 50-50 to build a panel plant: incorporated JV. Two engineering firms sign a contract to jointly bid and build one bridge: contractual JV.

JV vs partnership vs consortium

  • Partnership (Queen City Law): partners are jointly liable, can bind each other, and owe fiduciary duties.
  • JV: parties usually hold assets as tenants in common, are severally (individually) liable, and cannot bind each other.
  • Consortium (Pakistan Engineering Council): each member stays individually liable for its own part, while in a JV members indemnify each other and split liability.

Because no contract can foresee everything, a JV needs more than one way to keep partners aligned. Calle calls these governance mechanisms.

How JVs Are Governed
Calle (2018)

Three governance mechanisms

  1. The contract: the main up-front mechanism, which also sets up the other two.
  2. Equity share.
  3. The board or management committee: monitors and manages the JV and aligns interests.

Relational mechanisms (trust, relationships) also matter. Contracts are always incomplete (Hart and Moore), so boards and relationships fill the gaps.

Transaction cost economics (TCE): firms pick structures that minimize the cost and risk of dealing with others; JVs are hybrids between markets and hierarchies. Other lenses: resource-based view, organizational learning, institutional, agency, and resource dependence theories.

Three levels of governance decisions

  1. Governance mode: make, buy, or ally.
  2. Type of alliance: non-equity vs equity (contractual, minority equity, JV).
  3. Internal governance design (where Calle's study sits).

In the Philippines, a JV can also be a merger in the eyes of competition law. The PCC guidelines say when it must be reported.

PCC Guidelines on Notification of JVs (2018)
Philippine rules

Issued under Sec. 17 of RA 10667 (Philippine Competition Act). JVs are treated as mergers and must be notified if thresholds are met. The JV partners are the "acquiring entities" and the JV is the "acquired entity." The concern is substantial lessening of competition.

Three ways to form a JV

  1. Incorporate a JV company.
  2. Enter a contractual JV.
  3. Acquire shares in an existing corporation (a JV only if joint control results).

Thresholds (2018 values)

  • Size of party: the ultimate parent entity (UPE) of at least one party has Philippine gross revenues or assets above PHP 5 billion.
  • Size of transaction: assets combined or contributed in the Philippines (or the revenues they generate) above PHP 2 billion. Count agreed transfers, credit and guarantees, deferred contributions, and contributions within 1 year. No minimum shareholding is needed for joint control.

The PCC has adjusted these thresholds since 2018, so know them as the values in the reading.

Joint control

Joint control is the ability to substantially influence or direct the JV's actions. It can be positive (able to decide strategic matters) or negative (able to veto them), and de jure or de facto. Forms:

  1. Equal voting rights (two partners, equal votes, no casting vote; or equal board appointments).
  2. Veto rights over strategic decisions: key officers, budget, business plan, market-critical decisions. One such veto may be enough.
  3. Joint exercise of voting rights by minority shareholders (holding company, pooling agreement, or strong common interests).
  4. Other analogous cases.
Vetoes over sale or winding-up, changes to the articles, capital increases or decreases are normally not enough for joint control. They only protect a minority investor.
The ITC Model Corporate JV
ITC (2010)

Key features

  1. 50-50 ownership.
  2. Initial contribution; further funding only by mutual consent.
  3. JVC formed in one jurisdiction; its articles must match the contract.
  4. An agreed business plan.
  5. Ancillary contracts for assets, technology, services, supply, distribution.
  6. The board directs, but Reserved Matters need both parties.
  7. Shares sold only with mutual consent.
  8. Ending the JV usually needs agreement, but a party may call for winding up after the deadlock procedure.

Board rules

Board of 4 (2 per party); meets at least quarterly; quorum needs at least one director from each side; the chairman has no casting vote; resolutions need at least one vote from each side.

Reserved Matters (14)

New shares; changes to articles; sale of the JVC (these three are shareholder matters); borrowing above a limit; annual budget; new territory or products; major reorganization; large contracts; appointing the CEO; auditors and accounting policy; dividend policy changes; big litigation; payments to either party's group; insolvency filings.

Deadlock and termination

Refer to the chairmen or CEOs → notice to resolve within 90 days by (a) the JVC buying the unhappy party's shares, (b) the other party or a third party buying them, (c) selling the whole JVC, or (d) winding up → if not solved in 90 days, the unhappy party may demand winding up, but not within the first 2 years.

Other articles: dividend policy (e.g., distribute at least 50% of after-tax profit), share transfer only with consent, confidentiality, no competing business, force majeure (terminate after 3 months), hardship, and supremacy of the contract over the articles between the parties.

Part B. Negotiating Technology Transfer Agreements

Before learning how to negotiate, know the Philippine rules on who owns and can sell research results from government funding. Then the readings teach how to bargain fairly and well.

RA 10055: Philippine Technology Transfer Act of 2009
Philippine law

Key definitions

  • Technology transfer: the process by which one party systematically transfers to another the knowledge for making a product, applying a process, or rendering a service; may involve transfer, assignment, or licensing of IPRs.
  • Commercialization: earning income from a technology through spin-offs, licensing, or sale.
  • GFA (Government Funding Agency): a government agency or GOCC that funds research.
  • RDI (Research and Development Institute): a public or private R&D organization registered in the Philippines. Private RDIs must be at least 60% Filipino-owned.
  • Spin-off: an independent company created on the researcher's initiative to commercialize the technology.
  • Fairness Opinion Report (FOR): an independent third-party opinion on whether a directly negotiated deal is fair, especially its financial terms.
  • Technology Licensing Office (TLO): the RDI office that handles patents, licensing, valuation, and promotion.

Coverage: R&D by RDIs getting grants from GFAs; all IPRs from government-funded R&D; all agencies funding R&D; all institutions implementing it.

Sec. 6, Ownership

Who owns the IP

IP from GFA-funded research is owned by the RDI that actually performed the research. The GFA takes the rights only in four cases:

  1. The RDI voluntarily signs a public written agreement sharing, waiving, or assigning rights to the GFA to protect public interest (national security, nutrition, health, vital sectors).
  2. The RDI fails to disclose potential IPRs to the GFA.
  3. The RDI fails to start protection within a reasonable time, never more than 3 months from public disclosure.
  4. The RDI stops being a Filipino corporation.

For collaborative research, RDIs own jointly or as agreed. Employee IP rules under the IP Code still apply.

DOST funds a state university to develop a rice-drying technology. The university (the RDI that did the work) owns the patent, not DOST, unless one of the four exceptions applies, such as the university publishing a paper about it and not filing within 3 months.

Duties, revenue, spin-offs, and government use

  • GFA duties (Sec. 7): protect government interest through the funding agreement; monitor and help the RDI commercialize; keep IP free for further research and publication; allow revenue sharing that does not choke commercialization.
  • RDI duties (Sec. 8): identify, protect, manage, and diligently commercialize IP; notify the GFA of filings and licenses; report yearly; keep revenue accounts; give staff incentives; keep potential IPRs confidential; create spin-offs when needed.
  • Direct negotiation of commercialization by a public RDI or GFA needs a written DOST Secretary recommendation plus a Fairness Opinion Report.
  • Revenue sharing (Sec. 11): revenue goes to the RDI unless the funding agreement says otherwise, but the GFA's share can never exceed the RDI's. RDI-researcher sharing follows RA 8439 (Magna Carta for Scientists).
  • Spin-offs (Secs. 12 to 14): a researcher may create or work for a spin-off; leave of absence of 1 year, renewable once (maximum 2 years), keeping tenure and seniority. Conflict-of-interest rules: researchers stay accountable; those with a financial stake must not represent the RDI in dealings with the spin-off; those on spin-off boards must protect the RDI; collaborations need formal written public agreements.
  • Government use (Secs. 15 to 17): for national emergency or public interest, the GFA head recommends within 30 days, the parent agency decides within 30 days; the RDI may oppose within 15 days; profits are shared; rights go back to the RDI when the cause ends. Only the Supreme Court may issue TROs or injunctions against it.
  • Revolving fund (Sec. 18): income funds IP management, R&D, capability building, and TLOs, not salaries or allowances. If income after costs exceeds 10% of the RDI's annual budget, at least 70% of the excess goes to the Treasury (only if the GFA solely funded the research; not for SUCs and GOCCs with fiscal autonomy).
  • Other: DOST technology information facility; RDIs encouraged to set up TLOs; DOST, DTI, and IPO issue valuation guidelines; a Technology Transfer Oversight Committee of 5 Senators and 5 Representatives.

RA 10055 states the rules. DOST AO 009 turns them into a step-by-step procedure for DOST research institutes.

Technology Generator = the RDI and its researchers. Technology Maker = the researchers directly involved. Technology Adoptor = the party taking up the technology.

Timeline

From disclosure to filing

  1. RDI signs a Research Agreement with researchers (disclose, assign IP to the RDI, revenue share, confidentiality).
  2. Researchers submit prior art or patent search results.
  3. Researchers file an Invention Disclosure Form; the TLO assigns a case number; researchers sign a Deed of Assignment to the RDI.

  5. RDI files with IPOPHL (through TAPI) within 30 calendar days of disclosure to the GFA.
  6. If a public disclosure happens, file within 3 months.
  7. Notify the GFA of filing within 3 months; report yearly.

Trade-secret protection is an option if the GFA agrees. Marketing tools prepared by the TLO: technology package, business plan, brochures, user's guide, training manual.

Readiness: a Technical Review Committee checks that the patent application is accepted or granted (if patentable) and that R&D is complete.

Choosing an adoptor: (a) legal existence and track record, (b) financial capability, (c) compliance with legal and commercialization requirements.

  2. Capacity building and technology transfer

  2. Modified total direct costs basis: excludes items like equipment, patient care, off-site rent, scholarships.

Now to the negotiation itself. Sorensen et al. treat it as both a science you prepare for and an art you practice.

Negotiation as Science
Fisher and Ury; Sorensen et al.

Positional bargaining vs principled negotiation

Positional bargaining means each side digs into a stance. It can be soft or hard, and the hard game beats the soft one. It produces unwise deals.

US-USSR test-ban talks broke down over the number of on-site inspections: the Soviets offered 3, the US wanted 10. Both sides argued numbers instead of discussing what inspections were meant to achieve.

Principled negotiation (Harvard Negotiation Project) is "hard on the merits, soft on the people." Its four points:

  1. People: separate the people from the problem.
  2. Interests: focus on interests, not positions.
  3. Options: invent options for mutual gain.
  4. Criteria: insist on objective criteria.

A wise agreement meets legitimate interests, resolves conflicts fairly, lasts, and considers community interests.

BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement) is your best fallback if talks fail. It is the yardstick for judging offers, more flexible than a rigid bottom line, and the main source of negotiating power. If they are more powerful, develop your BATNA; if they won't play, use "negotiation jujitsu" and a one-text procedure; if they use dirty tricks, recognize, raise, and negotiate the rules.

A hospital negotiating a software license has a BATNA of "renew the old system for one more year at PHP 2M." Any new offer worse than that should be rejected, whatever the vendor says.

Sorensen et al.: prepare, listen, learn

Research the other party; listen first; do not bring a prepared contract to the first meeting; be personable ("never start a negotiation with a negotiation"), sincere, attentive to non-verbal cues, professional, brief, organized, and sensitive to cultural norms.

License terms most often negotiated (in order)

Parties → license scope (exclusive patent rights with non-exclusive know-how; fields of use; transferability) → conditions on the grant (reserved rights to research and publish; government rights) → licensed products → consideration (royalty base, third-party royalty offsets usually capped at 50%, milestones, sublicense share, audits; diligence is the heart) → risk management (confidentiality, patent costs, infringement, warranties, indemnity, insurance, termination and cure periods, dispute resolution).

Negotiation as Art: Ten Keys (Sorensen et al.)
  1. You can't have what you don't ask for, but you won't get everything you ask for.
  2. Psych out the deal and the personalities.
  3. Feel and mold the range of reasonableness (ROR): the zone between low-ball and high-ball offers in the other side's mind. Ask at its edge; outside it, you trigger outrage.
  4. Select your opening style, then adapt. Styles: confrontational, competitive, intellectual, collaborative, emotional, passive, wounded dove. Collaborative and intellectual are easiest to change later.
  5. Everything is tied to everything else (a low upfront fee means higher royalties or milestones).
  6. Trust your gut, but seek validation.
  7. Recognize and block dirty tricks: righteous indignation, good cop/bad cop, the high hat, "I'm not authorized," wounded dove, the nibble, the leapfrog, the mulligan, the policy play, selective benchmarks, false deadlines.
  8. Keep the power to back up and turn around (nothing is final until signed; keep another licensee in hand).
  9. When in a jam, try something exciting (e.g., a royalty cap in exchange for a share of a future acquisition price).
  10. Don't let the perfect fight the good enough.
"The nibble": after everything is agreed, the other side says, "Oh, and you'll include free training for 20 staff, right?" Block it by asking what they will give in return.

Modern thinking frames buying and selling technology as "open innovation." Tajudeen et al. test whether it actually pays.

Open Innovation (Tajudeen et al., 2019)
Definitions

Open innovation (OI) is "a distributed innovation process based on purposively managed knowledge flows across organizational boundaries" (Chesbrough and Bogers). It is the opposite of closed, do-everything-yourself innovation.

  • External technology acquisition (ETA) = inbound OI: bringing in outside technology and ideas (buying technology, crowdsourcing, contests, JVs, R&D alliances, university licensing). For resource-poor firms it is a double-edged sword because it can crowd out internal R&D.
  • External technology exploitation (ETE) = outbound OI: selling or licensing your technology to others. Benefits are financial (license income funds R&D) and strategic (focus on the core).
  • Technology scouting: systematic scanning of trends to spot opportunities and threats, here through social media.
  • Digitalization vision: a clear, shared view of the role of digital technology in strategy.

If buying technology is not automatically good, when is a joint arrangement better than a simple purchase? Kani and Motohashi answer this using the type of project.

Bilateral vs Unilateral Contracts (Kani and Motohashi, 2016)

Three sourcing alternatives

  1. Bilateral contract-based alliance: joint R&D or JV. Two-way flows, much interaction, real learning. Suits exploration.
  2. Unilateral contract-based alliance: licensing or commissioned R&D. Technology for cash at arm's length. Fast and flexible but limited learning.
  3. Not sourcing: fully in-house.

Exploration vs exploitation (March, 1991): exploration is trying new alternatives; exploitation is refining what you already do well.

Contractual hazards (from TCE) are the risks of dealing with outsiders: asset specificity (investments that lock you in, risking hold-up) and appropriability concerns (know-how leaking out).

Hypotheses and findings
  • H1: exploratory projects use bilateral contracts; as exploitation rises, firms shift to unilateral contracts, then in-house.
  • H2: higher absorptive capacity means more external sourcing.
  • H3: low contractual hazards favor unilateral contracts; as hazards rise, firms move to bilateral, then in-house (Oxley's continuum: unilateral → bilateral → equity alliances → in-house).

Data: RIETI survey, 1,199 Japanese new-product projects. Bilateral contracts are used for exploratory projects, in-house for exploitation, unilateral where hazards are low. Bilateral sourcing seemed to produce more "new-to-the-world" innovation, but the gap vanished after controlling for project type and managers. External sourcing is "not a panacea"; it works only under the right conditions.

Two theories: TCE (minimize production plus transaction costs) and the resource-based view (maximize value by pooling resources).

A Philippine food firm exploring a brand-new plant-based protein would co-develop with a university lab (bilateral). Improving the shelf life of an existing canned product would be done in-house. Buying a standard sterilization process would be licensed (unilateral).
Sector Differences (Kim and Kwon, 2011)

Using the Korea Innovation Survey 2005 (1,591 manufacturing firms), the authors reduced 67 variables to 11 factors and ran a regression per sector, based on a modified Pavitt taxonomy: supplier-dominated (resource or labor intensive), production-intensive (scale-intensive and specialized suppliers), and science-based (IT, BT, NT).

  • Whole industry: acquisition driven by external information sources (from firms and from universities), acquisition sources, government support, collaborative partners, and firm size.
  • External information from firms and markets was positive in every sector except biotech.
  • Supplier-dominated (small, weak R&D): government support and outside information matter most.
  • Science-based: university and institute information matters for BT and NT, not IT.

Lesson: drivers are sector-specific, so government policy and company strategy should be too.

Part B. Reverse Engineering Methods in Technology Acquisition

Reverse engineering was option 4 on UNIDO's list and Rani's "fastest route" in Module 2. Part B explains how it actually works.

What Reverse Engineering Is
Definitions

Reverse engineering (RE) is "a systematic process that extracts design information from an existing product" (Geng and Bidanda). Kumar adds that it creates engineering design data from existing parts. In law it is "starting with the known product and working backward to divine the process" (Kewanee Oil v. Bicron, 1974).

Forward engineering goes concept → CAD → part. Reverse engineering goes part → data → CAD model.

A local machine shop has a worn-out pump impeller from a 1980s imported machine with no drawings. It scans the impeller, rebuilds a CAD model, fixes the worn edges, and machines a new one.

Four reasons to reverse engineer (Geng and Bidanda)

  1. Design new products (from physical prototypes, clay models, turbine shapes).
  2. Modify existing products.
  3. Recover lost design data.
  4. Verify a product (compare it with its CAD model).

Reddy adds: replicate parts, restore damaged or worn parts, improve model accuracy, inspect digital models.

The basic steps

Geng and Bidanda's two steps: (1) move the object from the physical to the digital world (digitization); (2) connect it to other manufacturing processes (clean the data, fit surfaces, build the CAD model). In practice it loops.

Reddy's three phases:

1 Scanning→ 2 Point processing→ 3 CAD model generation (hardest)

Kumar's detailed steps: data acquisition → pre-processing (noise filtering, merging) → triangulation → feature extraction → segmentation and surface fitting → CAD/CAM/CAE use → export (IGES, STEP, STL; the parametric history is lost).

Kumar's three digital convergences: 1970s sound (1D), 1980s fonts and pictures (2D), 1990s physical objects (3D geometry).

Trade-off: contact is accurate but slow; non-contact is fast (minutes) with accuracy of about 0.01 to 1 mm, but cannot capture hollow structures or transparent surfaces. Lasers struggle with reflective materials and steep surfaces.

From Point Cloud to CAD Model
  • A point cloud is the mass of measured (x, y, z) coordinates ("a sea of coordinates") that must be cleaned, merged, and converted.
  • Delaunay triangulation builds a triangle mesh from the points. The result is often saved as an STL file, the link between RE, CAD, and 3D printing.
  • Segmentation splits the mesh into regions, each belonging to one simple surface.
  • Surface models: polygon meshes (simple but bulky, may have holes); algebraic surfaces (efficient, but a change means refitting everything); parametric surfaces such as Bezier, B-spline, and NURBS (easy to modify, robust, heavy computation).
  • Solid models can be used for finite element analysis, flow analysis, and NC programming.

Reddy's key process parameters: noise reduction level, percentage of points, percentage of triangles, and smoothing level. These drive surface deviation, processing time, memory, and defects. Surface reconstruction is an ill-posed problem: there can be more than one valid answer.

Tolerance stacking: manufacturing error + RE error + 3D-printing error can push a copied part outside its design tolerance. This is the main unsolved problem (Geng and Bidanda). Future directions: smart scanners, automated process planning, and a "3D copier."

A bracket is designed at 20.00 mm ± 0.10 mm. The original is made at 20.06 mm, the scan adds 0.03 mm error, and printing adds 0.04 mm. The copy can be 20.13 mm, out of tolerance even though each step looked fine.
Kumar et al.: applications and lessons

Mechanical (turbine blades, gears, engine parts); aerospace and ship hulls (legacy parts without CAD; Boeing digital spares); software (IEEE Std 1219-1993: "extracting software system information from source code"); medical (dentistry, hearing aids, implants); also accident reconstruction, fashion, architecture.

History: the Soviet Tu-4 was copied from three captured B-29s. Failures happen too (a 2003 Wright Flyer replica failed). Success needs attention to detail, accurate measurement, and understanding of the part's function and operating environment.

Beyond copying shapes, RE is also a way for developing countries to catch up, as the supplemental readings show.

Is RE Legal? (Samuelson and Scotchmer, 2001)

RE is a long-accepted, generally lawful way to learn from products bought legitimately, even to compete. It is the main way trade secrets enter the public domain. But each industry treats it differently:

The L Corporation Case (Laspinas and Posadas, 2015)
Philippine case

L Corporation is a Philippine broadcast company. It is a technology user, not a creator; its core competency is content creation. It acquired an Enterprise Service Management System (ESMS): a single ticketing platform to consolidate IT service management across group companies, track IT assets and licenses, improve availability and security, and automate workflows.

Eskelin and Herron explain each of these documents in more detail. Start with the most important one: the business need.

Key Documents and Roles (Eskelin; Herron)
Definitions
  • Business need: "a defined change to the current state of a business that will benefit the business in some way," either an opportunity or a problem. It states the change without naming a solution. Validate it with facts and list assumptions.
  • Business case: one package showing rationale, goals, benefits, costs and revenue, and risks. It wins resources and protects the project from second-guessing.
  • Project charter: defines what the project is: business need, objective, impact (benefits of doing; consequences of not doing), success measures, scope ("draw a box"), risks, time constraints, approvals. It should rarely change. The project plan covers how, when, where, and who.
Eskelin's Jack Smith case: a team assumed users needed a faster screen (GUI) and bought one. It failed because the real problem was how the knowledge base was organized. Stating a solution as the "need" sent the project the wrong way.
Good vs bad need statement: "Buy a new ticketing software" names a solution. "Cut IT ticket resolution time from 3 days to 1 day across all group companies" states the change.

Mortara and Ford step back from the project mechanics to the strategic context: why acquire, from whom, and how mature the technology is.

Acquisition Context (Mortara and Ford, Section 1)

Technology acquisition = bringing in technology from outside instead of developing it through your own R&D. The process: identification → assessment (selection and terms) ⇄ negotiation → transfer. Assess before negotiating, because people who negotiate without clear objectives decide emotionally.

Question 1: Why acquire? Four motives

  1. Develop new technological capabilities: fill R&D gaps, create new products, overcome technology exhaustion.
  2. Increase strategic options: counter inertia, open new markets, keep options open under uncertainty.
  3. Gain efficiency: faster time to market, lower cost (fixed costs become variable).
  4. Respond to the competitive environment: hostile markets, rapid change, fast competitors.

A checklist weights each motive 0 to 10 to set priorities and remove personal bias.

Question 2: From whom?

  • Universities: keen on commercialization but inexperienced; publish-vs-patent tension; staff turnover means leaks.
  • Start-ups: idea-rich but short of resources; VC influence; flexible but volatile; overprotective of one technology.
  • Private companies (suppliers, customers, competitors, consultancies), individuals, and government agencies.
  • Consortia (one-to-many), for complex problems: pre-competitive (share risk), policy-instigated (often weak motivation), supply-chain or association-sponsored (need a champion).

Question 3: How mature is it?

Use NASA's Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs) or the STAM model: Science → Technology → Application → Market.

Narrowing down the options

Cross technology readiness with market readiness. A "buy" scenario (both ready) means de-risk by buying a firm, licensing, or a JV. A "speculative" scenario (market not ready) suits pre-competitive consortia. A "game-changer" needs internal reconfiguration and absorptive capacity. Research-stage technology for a new market points to a university partnership or partial equity.

Speser looks at the same deal from the seller's side, as a game. Understanding how sellers think helps a buyer negotiate.

The Game of Technology Transfer (Speser, Ch. 1 to 3)
Ch. 1: The pieces

Technology is an aid for a repeated activity, existing "where thought precedes action." A deal happens when the goods exchanged are roughly equal in value to both sides.

Three factors that set value

  • Desirability: how well it meets the buyer's criteria. "Never value a technology on why it is desirable to you."
  • Attachment: how badly one wants it or how easily one parts with it. "Never value technology on its development costs."
  • Substitutes: alternatives that set the fair price. "Never value a technology above its substitutes unless you have brand loyalty."

Utility = weighted performance + ease of use. Net utility = benefits minus (price + changeover and operating costs). Deals happen where a technology gives buyers superior net utility. Changeover costs (training, new procedures) explain why buyers often wait until the old technology is depreciated.

A dominant design is the combination of functions and features users come to expect. A practice is a whole activity built from tasks and task sequences (dribbling → fast-break lay-up → basketball).

A grain-moisture sensor using an MRI magnet was far more accurate than farmers needed and too expensive. Over-performance lost the sale; net utility was negative.
Ch. 2: The board

Technology transfer is usually a push game, so finding buyers is the core challenge. The arena has five parts: users, buyers, competitors, markets, stakeholders. Map it by calling stakeholders until the answers repeat (5 to 7 calls).

  • Supply chains: integrators push R&D to suppliers and specify performance, not technology. Get the top integrator's endorsement, then work down.
  • Strategy shapes what buyers want: cost leaders want incremental improvements; differentiators want mature extensions; focused firms want prototyped or mature products. Decision power sits in marketing/sales or the relevant practice, not in legal or R&D.
  • Competitors: anyone who could sell a substitute within 5 years.
  • Tech-transfer markets are specialized and diffuse, so use targeted research and phone calls.
Ch. 3: Strategies
"Sell customers, not technology."

Three critical questions: (1) Can we find an application (a technology niche)? (2) Can we find a deal vehicle that works for us? (3) Can we make it a Schelling point for a partner?

A Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies where no player gains by changing alone. Acceptable deals are Nash equilibria; contracts freeze them. A Schelling (focal) point is the equilibrium both sides naturally pick.

SMART (simple multi-attribute rating technique), 8 steps: identify decision makers → criteria → scales → rank criteria → rate them → normalize into weights → score each option × weight → sum.

Choosing a deal vehicle depends on control (from non-exclusive license up to selling all rights), the partner's absorptive capacity (domain and context expertise), and assets. "With everything, no partner; with nothing, sell the technology."

Part B. Technical Risk Assessment in Technology Acquisition

The business case lists risks. Part B shows how to assess them properly, using two models: NIST (information security) and DSTO (defense acquisition).

What Risk Is
Definitions

NIST: risk is "a measure of the extent to which an entity is threatened by a potential circumstance or event," typically a function of (i) the adverse impact if it occurs and (ii) the likelihood it will occur.

DSTO (AS/NZS ISO 31000): risk is "the effect of uncertainty on objectives." Risk assessment = risk identification + risk analysis + risk evaluation.

Risk vs issue (DSTO): a risk might happen; an issue has happened or certainly will. No uncertainty means it is an issue, not a risk.

A thermal imager designed for northern Europe will not work in a tropical rainforest because humidity absorbs infrared. That is certain, so it is a fitness-for-purpose issue, reported separately; it does not change the overall risk rating.
NIST SP 800-30: Building Blocks

Risk management vs risk assessment

Risk management process (4 components)Risk assessment process (4 steps)
Frame → Assess → Respond → MonitorPrepare → Conduct → Communicate → Maintain
Do not mix these two lists. Frame/assess/respond/monitor is risk management; prepare/conduct/communicate/maintain is risk assessment.

The risk frame is the set of assumptions, constraints, risk tolerances, and priorities behind the risk strategy. Assess identifies threats, vulnerabilities, harm, and likelihood. Respond develops, evaluates, chooses, and implements courses of action. Monitor checks effectiveness and changes.

Risk factors

  • Threat: any circumstance or event that could harm operations, assets, or people through an information system.
  • Threat source: adversarial (individuals, groups, organizations, nation-states), accidental (users, admins), structural (equipment, environmental controls, software failure), environmental (natural or man-made disasters, infrastructure outages).
  • Threat event vs threat scenario (a sequence of events that "tells a story").
  • Threat shifting: attackers change plans when they see defenses, in four domains: time, target, resource, attack method.
  • Vulnerability: a weakness a threat could exploit, in systems, procedures, governance, supply chains, or architecture.
  • Predisposing condition: a condition that increases or decreases the likelihood of harm.
  • Likelihood: combines the chance an event starts and the chance it causes harm, tied to a time frame.
  • Impact: the size of harm from unauthorized disclosure, change, destruction, or loss of availability.
A data center on a flood plain is a predisposing condition that raises likelihood. A stand-alone computer with no network lowers it. A system with no database cannot suffer SQL injection.

Approaches and tiers

Assessment approachDescription
QuantitativeNumbers; best for cost-benefit; can be costly and hard to interpret

Analysis approaches: threat-oriented (start from threats), asset/impact-oriented (start from critical assets), vulnerability-oriented (start from weaknesses). The starting point can bias results, so use a second orientation.

Three tiers: Tier 1 Organization (strategic), Tier 2 Mission/Business Process, Tier 3 Information System (tactical). Traditional assessments over-focus on Tier 3. Tier 1 assessments inform IT investment and procurement decisions.

Uncertainty comes from: the future not resembling the past, incomplete threat knowledge, undiscovered vulnerabilities, and unrecognized dependencies.

4 steps, 13 tasks

The NIST risk assessment process

Step 1, Prepare: identify (1) purpose, (2) scope, (3) assumptions and constraints, (4) information sources, (5) risk model and analytic approach.

Step 2, Conduct (in order):

  1. Identify and characterize threat sources.
  2. Identify threat events.
  3. Identify vulnerabilities and predisposing conditions.
  4. Determine likelihood.
  5. Determine impact.
  6. Determine risk (likelihood + impact, with uncertainty).

Step 3, Communicate: share results with decision makers; share risk information with staff.

Step 4, Maintain: monitor risk factors; update the assessment.

Semi-quantitative scale: Very High 96 to 100; High 80 to 95; Moderate 21 to 79; Low 5 to 20; Very Low 0 to 4. Risk is usually no higher than the impact level, unless many moderate risks add up.

NIST thinks about information systems. DSTO applies risk thinking to buying complex equipment, which is closer to technology acquisition.

DSTO: Technology Risk vs Technical Risk
Background

The handbook exists because of acquisition failures. The Kinnaird Review (2003), after problems with the Collins submarine, required analysis of technology, cost, and schedule risks, with the Chief Defence Scientist giving independent advice. The Pappas Review (2008) found technical risk was the main cause of schedule slips.

Four basic questions: Is the technology feasible? Will it mature in time? Are there barriers to integrating it? Is it fit for purpose?

The TRI is an early, high-level check (prepared by the Project Science and Technology Adviser, approved by the Lead Chief of Division). The TRA is the detailed assessment. The TRC (Technical Risk Certification) is the Chief Defence Scientist's statement to Government, using the TRA.

Five steps of a TRA

  1. Establish the context of use and objectives (system boundaries, external conditions, dependencies).
  2. Identify the sub-systems (based on the Work Breakdown Structure).
  3. Assess technology risks (rate TRLs; anything below TRL 9 is a possible risk).
  4. Assess technical risks (rate SRLs; anything below SRL 9 is a possible risk).
  5. Determine the overall risk level.

Unknowns: either assume the worst case (More Than Likely), or assess the range across candidate solutions. Confidence is reported as high, medium, or low.

Mortara and Ford: Acquisition Evaluation
Three evaluation factors
  1. Your ability to absorb and use the technology (absorptive capacity).
  2. Compatibility of you and your partner.
  3. Suitability of the technology.

Absorptive capacity here is "the ability of the firm to evaluate, appropriate and make good use of external knowledge." It depends on technical knowledge, acquisition experience, own R&D, related IP, willingness to accept outside ideas, flexibility, internal buy-in, knowledge sharing, and ability to apply it in many ways. It grows through education, training, and experience.

Not-invented-here (NIH) syndrome: in-house staff rejecting outside ideas.

Know-how / know-what / know-who: skills and the ability to use them / specific technical and market facts (manuals, procedures) / knowing which experts and organizations can make it work. Since tacit knowledge lives in people's heads, keeping key personnel matters.

Partner compatibility covers shared strategic vision, compatible strategies, mutual dependence, market acceptance, and technical capability, plus working style, structure, distance, pace (big firm vs start-up), and culture. Due diligence checks the partner's background before committing.

Checklist mechanics

Three checklists: absorb (9 items), partner compatibility (10 items), technology suitability (9 items).

  • Column B: weight from 0 (no importance) to 10.
  • Column C: score from -3 (low) to +3 (high).
  • Column D: weight × score, then summed.
  • Box E: total of weights × 3, the maximum possible score.
Weight 8 × score +2 = 16. Weight 5 × score -1 = -5. If all weights total 59, Box E = 177, so the axis runs from -177 to +177. Mortara's worked example scored 78 out of ±177.

Seeing the Market Like a Seller (Speser, Ch. 4 to 6)
Ch. 4: Finding the customer

Four steps: (1) what we have to sell, (2) which practices offer applications, (3) who the end users are, (4) who the buyers are.

  • A technology is a bundle of functionality, characteristics (complexity, scalability, adaptability, packaging, fragility, trialability, platform relationships), and features.
  • Potential = maximum feasible performance; yield = current performance; the gap is stretch.
  • End user vs buyer: end users "put their hands on" it and want utility; buyers purchase it and want value. Pull-through is when end users love it so much they push their buyers to purchase it.
  • Four buyer types: price buyers, value buyers (lowest lifetime cost), availability buyers, technical performance buyers.
  • Bohn's 8 knowledge levels: ignorance, awareness, measure, control of the mean, control of variance ("cookbook"), process characterization, scientific model, complete knowledge (unattainable).
  • "Without test results, it is meaningless to claim a TRL."
  • Rogers' adopters: innovators (~3%), early adopters (~14%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%), laggards (16%). Take-off at about 5% to 10% penetration.
  • Difficult-to-use products are often better sold as services.

IP forms: patents (20 years from filing; new and non-obvious), trade secrets (no registration; no protection against independent invention), trademarks (last while used), copyrights (author's life + 70 years; protects expression, not idea), mask works (10 years). Before a deal: "say what it does, not how it does it."

Ch. 5: Competing technology

Four questions: (1) Are we redundant, and what competes? (2) How is the rival positioned against the dominant design? (3) Who sells the substitutes? (4) Who else might emerge?

  • Competitive advantage: meeting end-user needs better than others; the most important success factor.
  • Competitive range: competitors at or above the end user's baseline; below it, they are redundant.
  • Swamping: a substitute suddenly shortens the period a product can be sold.
  • Hurdle rate: the minimum return needed to do a deal.
  • Gray literature: non-peer-reviewed sources such as government R&D reports. Latent needs: needs not yet stated.
Ch. 6: Markets

Barriers are like mass: facts that make buying patterns resist change. Forces are like vectors: measurable tendencies that change buying behavior. "If you can't measure it, it ain't a force." A regulation by itself is a barrier; the pressure to comply is the force.

Part B. Technology Procurement in the Public Sector

A private firm can design its own process. A Philippine government agency must follow the procurement law. The law changed in 2024, so the readings ask for a critical comparison of the old and new laws.

The Legal Framework
Philippine law
  • RA 9184, Government Procurement Reform Act, approved 10 January 2003. Built one competitive, transparent system with public bidding as the default.
  • RA 12009, New Government Procurement Act, approved 20 July 2024, in force 13 August 2024. Its IRR took effect 25 February 2025. It repealed RA 9184 and replaced it with a "fit-for-purpose" system. RA 9184's rules applied until the new IRR took effect, with a 3-year transition from approval of standard forms.

Key terms

  • Procurement: acquiring goods and consulting services and contracting infrastructure projects by a Procuring Entity; includes leases.
  • Procuring Entity (PE) and its head (HoPE), who approves the award.
  • Bids and Awards Committee (BAC): runs the procurement and recommends the award, with a Technical Working Group (TWG) and Secretariat.
  • ABC (Approved Budget for the Contract): the ceiling for bids; there is no floor.
  • PPMP (end-user's plan) is consolidated into the APP (Annual Procurement Plan). No procurement outside the APP.
  • GPPB: the central policy board. PhilGEPS: the single e-procurement portal. PS-DBM: central buyer of common-use supplies.
  • Value for money: the best mix of quality and cost. Lifecycle cost: total cost of ownership (acquire, operate, maintain, dispose).
  • Beneficial owner: the real person who ultimately owns or controls a bidding company.
  • Jury duty: BAC, TWG, and Secretariat give procurement top priority over their other work until done.

Eight governing principles (RA 12009, Sec. 3)

  1. Transparency
  2. Competitiveness
  3. Efficiency
  4. Proportionality (requirements and mode matched to the need)
  5. Accountability
  6. Participatory procurement (public monitoring)
  7. Sustainability
  8. Professionalism

RA 9184 had five: transparency, competitiveness, streamlined process, accountability, public monitoring.

Strategic planning: market scoping, lifecycle cost analysis, framework agreements, pooled procurement, procurement agents, early procurement. Specifications must describe characteristics or performance; brand names are banned except for technical compatibility, interoperability, servicing, maintenance, or warranty, with written justification.

Why Reforms Struggled
Jones (2010)

RA 9184 had six reform aims: a more competitive system, better planning and budgeting, transparency and standardization (observers, PhilGEPS from 2007), quality (consulting scored 60% to 85% technical, 15% to 40% fee), anti-corruption (prison terms of 6 to 15 years), and watchdogs (COA, Ombudsman, GPPB).

Evidence of failure: worse World Bank corruption scores (-0.31 in 1996 to -0.75 in 2008); 2008 survey median bribe of 15% of contract value; clustered bids showing collusion. Reasons: loopholes, weak watchdogs, weak bid-protest system (fee of at least 1% of ABC), fading CSO observers, under-used PhilGEPS.

Two root causes: a culture of informal influence, and elite capture by political-business-family networks.
Navarro and Tanghal (2017)

The problem was underspending: PHP 302.7B below program in 2014 and PHP 328.3B in 2015. Some proposed making alternative modes the default to speed things up.

PhilGEPS data (2012 to 2015) disagreed: in civil works, alternative modes failed more often (59%) than competitive bidding (47%). GPPB's causes of delay: poor planning 50%, too few bidders 22%, approval and review 14%, lack of manpower 6%.

Other findings: BAC work is an add-on duty; "lowest responsive" makes it hard to buy quality (PAGASA and MIRDC picked more responsive, not cheapest, bidders for Doppler radars and CNC spindles); highly technical R&D items attract few bidders.

Absorptive capacity here means an agency's ability to actually spend its budget (DSWD's budget jumped from about PHP 1.5 to 2B to PHP 102B).

Four recommendations

  1. Political will and investment in systems and culture (DPWH under Singson cut documents from 20 to 5).
  2. More investment in planning and preparation.
  3. Innovation orientation (strategic procurement, e.g., DOST Project NOAH).
  4. Value-for-money procurement (quality, fit, track record, whole-of-life cost).

Linking the readings to RA 12009

RA 12009 answers many of these criticisms: MEARB addresses "lowest bid doesn't buy quality"; strategic planning and market scoping address poor planning; professionalization addresses capacity; DP-STI addresses innovation; beneficial ownership and e-protests address collusion and weak protests. But Jones's root causes (informal influence, elite capture) are cultural, and a new law alone cannot remove them.

L Corporation: Implementation
Philippine case

Implementation steps (in order)

  1. Form an internal roll-out team as a weak matrix (members keep their regular jobs; the PM is part-time with limited authority; functional heads decide).
  2. Kickoff with process owners, project team, end users, and the steering committee.
  3. Detailed data gathering for the Low-Level Design (LLD); this builds ownership.
  4. Business Requirement Document (BRD) sign-off before development; this builds ownership and controls scope.
  5. Knowledge transfer: internal IT staff join installation, unit testing, and system testing.
  6. SIT (System Integration Testing), then UAT (User Acceptance Testing), before production.
  7. Out-of-scope requests become Change Requests, with cost.
  8. UAT sign-off.
  9. End-user training.
SIT comes before UAT. SIT checks the new system works with existing systems; UAT checks it meets business needs. The BRD is signed before development.
After BRD sign-off, the finance team asks for a new report not in the BRD. Because the scope was signed, this becomes a change request with its own cost and time, instead of a "free" add-on that delays go-live.

Operation and absorption

Absorption means building technological capability, which has three elements:

  1. Embodied: in equipment and systems.
  2. Non-embodied: knowledge, skills, documentation.
  3. Organizational integration.

How L Corp secured absorption through the contract:

  • Knowledge Transfer (KT) agreement: hands-on immersion; fixed classroom training hours written into the contract; documentation (technical, functional, controls and policies).
  • Maintenance agreement: warranties, upgrades, bug fixes.
  • Handholding period after go-live, under vendor supervision, until the system is stable.
  • Refresher training; later roll-outs done by L Corp's own staff.

Results: go-live in December 2014 with no Severity 1 issue; success measured by KPIs set at Initiation; a communication plan and change management plan; phased roll-out with a pilot in IT first. Framed as organizational learning (Bell and Pavitt's learning-by-doing). L Corp used a sociocentric perspective, sits at the integrative/investment capability level, and acquires mainly by direct purchase.

Positioning for the End User (Speser, Ch. 7)

Strategy formulation has three steps: positioning (the goal state of successful market entry) → launch tactics (how to get there) → targets (who to deal with, Ch. 9).

  • What positioning promises: industrial goods → money-making potential; consumer goods → pleasure; public goods → risk reduction.
  • Take-off happens at about 5% to 20% penetration, driven by early adopters (opinion leaders, best-value buyers).
  • Umpf: the emotional, shared motivation that makes people want something. Industrial goods need two: the boss (money) and the worker (easier, safer job), or workers may sabotage it.
The zipper existed from 1851 and 1893 but failed for lack of umpf. It took off only in 1928, when shorter hemlines and fitted flapper dresses made it fashionable and designer Elsa Schiaparelli used colored zippers as a design element.

  1. Performance (repetitive task sequences are open to technology).
  2. Ease of use (depends on the skills users already have).
  3. Price: comparables, value generated, and sticker shock. Gordon Bell's price tiers rise by factors of ten: $10, $100, $1,000, $10,000, $100,000, $1M, $10M. Base royalties on gross profit; "more risk, higher rates."
  4. Time: competitive advantage is a moving target.

A window of opportunity opens when something changes in the practice. It closes through a new competing technology, saturation, too many vendors, or a paradigm change. Aim for a Nash equilibrium lasting at least 5 years.

Two jobs remain: closing a fair deal, and getting people to accept the change.

L Corporation: Negotiation
Philippine case
  1. Commercial bids stay sealed until technical evaluation ends. Only the top 3 are opened, simultaneously, with Project, Procurement, and Legal present; a tally sheet is signed.
  2. Two or more shortlisted vendors give leverage.
  3. Bids include the best offer, payment options, and value-adds (free training, support hours, consulting).
  4. Procurement equalizes the playing field: each vendor is asked to include the value-adds the others offered, for an apples-to-apples comparison.
  5. Negotiate with each vendor separately, without disclosing others' offers.
  6. Award to the most competitive and compelling offer.
Vendor A offers 40 free training hours; Vendor B offers 1 year of free support. Procurement asks both to quote including 40 training hours and 1 year of support, so their prices can be compared fairly.

Speser's Part Four follows the same sequence for any technology deal: who to deal with, how much to ask, and how to negotiate.

Finding the Target (Speser, Ch. 9)

The target is the licensee, investor, or partner. Three steps: it sells into your market (or can credibly enter it); it has absorptive capacity in your market's context; and its capabilities make up for your SWOT weaknesses. Alignment on market, technology, and capabilities makes the deal a Nash equilibrium for both.

  • Core / strategic / enabling technology: how players compete today (no one buys a competing core except to kill it) / how they want to compete tomorrow (they will want exclusivity) / what helps them use core and strategic technology (they want access if rivals have it).
  • Offensive vs defensive hedge: buying to exploit opportunities vs buying to keep a technology off the market. With a defensive buyer, get cash up front, since it may shelve your technology.
  • NETOUT / NETIN (Hanel) identify technology importers, who are better targets. Imitators (who cite others' patents) are more receptive than pioneers.
  • Risk attitude: risk loving, neutral, averse. Prefer minimax targets unless the technology is mature.
  • Use QFD's "house of quality" to define needed capabilities; check timing against the target's product cycle.
Valuing the Technology (Speser, Ch. 10)

A valuation is always a guesstimate of expected value, based on assumptions about revenue, expenses, and risk. Discounted cash flow (DCF) is the basis: future money is worth less today, so shrink future net cash flows back to present value.

Speser's example: invest $100, then receive $21, $34, $40, $33, $17 over five years. Discounted at about 7% to 8%, the NPV is about $17.42; adding inflation lowers it to $14.87.

Doing the Deal (Speser, Ch. 11)

A deal allocates the value a technology creates. A good deal makes breaking it cost more than keeping it, so supporting it is everyone's best strategy.

Planning: three questions

  1. What do I need?
  2. What must I give to get it (the asset package)?
  3. Is it worthwhile (minimum net benefit)?
  • The business reason for any deal is weakness: you cannot do or manage all the tasks yourself.
  • Magic-marker technique: color each task by the party best able to do it; tasks go to whoever does them best.
  • Your floor is the do-it-yourself baseline. If no scenario beats it, walk away.
  • The intellectual asset (IA) package includes IP plus drawings, manuals, software, databases, and know-how. Include everything needed and nothing more. Know-how matters most for process and service technologies.
  • Value of an IA = cost of the task without it - cost with it. Pay for it through side payments, and leave the other side some value.
  • Licensor cash flows: initiation fee, milestones, annual royalties, maintenance fee. Base the initiation fee on the buyer's risk reduction, not on "15% of development cost."
MEMC licensed silicon-wafer know-how to Posco Huls (1998) in stages: consultant, specs, engineering manual, drawings, operations manual and training, on-site engineers, then 5 years of mutual improvements. The $7.8M fee was paid in 12 quarterly installments plus a running royalty, so payment was tied to deliverables.

Negotiating

Negotiation is ultimately about trust. Build relationships years ahead. On the first call, give the value proposition and ask qualifying questions (interest, fit, criteria, decision-maker, willingness to test). Get them to commit to deadlines, not decisions.

Term sheet = a draft framework of key points, clearly labeled a discussion draft so it is not taken as an offer. Prepare with war gaming (mock negotiations).

Five rules

  1. Be fair: unfair deals fall apart.
  2. Put each burden on the party best able to control it. Never warrant that your patent is valid; only that you know of no reason it is invalid.
  3. Help the other side meet its hurdle rate.
  4. Be honest.
  5. Follow the agenda: memos summarizing agreements → consensus document → lawyers.

To break a deadlock, use the royalty calculator: an industry-norm rate adjusted by Georgia-Pacific factors. Fallback order: meets everyone's needs → fair market value → split the difference. After making an offer, stay quiet; let the other side save face.

The deal is done. Now the people side: technology succeeds only when its technical, human, and organizational sides are managed together.

Change Management (Mlekus et al., 2018)

Change management: the approaches and actions needed to design and carry out change successfully. An organization is a sociotechnical system: a social system (people, attitudes) and a technical system (tools, procedures) that depend on each other.

Why sociotechnical: a meta-analysis of 131 field studies found that changes addressing technology, people, and organization together had bigger effects than single-focus changes.

Why employee participation works

  1. Fairness: having a say feels just and builds commitment.
  2. Autonomy: employees align the change with their own values, reducing stress.
  3. Job crafting: employees shape their jobs to be more meaningful.

The instrument: three dimensions

Human (6 criteria, e.g., abilities needed, qualification, autonomy, social interaction, work diversity), Technology (10, e.g., maturity, compatibility, means of interaction, flexibility, complexity, cost, safety, security), and Organization (9, e.g., time-to-market, labor, competencies, process impact, implementation effort, collaboration, agility).

Tested on three digital-work scenarios: a mixed mock-up with augmented reality, a conformity database, and digital documentation of production. N = 24 experts in Germany; inter-rater agreement (ICC) 0.94. The scenarios differed on all three dimensions, so a sociotechnical approach is necessary. Limitations: small sample, experts rather than real users, validity not yet established.

Practical value: compare options at a glance, let HR prepare training before implementation, and involve employees so they accept the change.

Five success factors

  1. Planning: a clear vision and documented case for change.
  2. Defined governance: Steering Committee (approves budget, scope, risk) → Change Sponsor (senior, ultimately accountable, usually chairs the committee) → Change Agent (day-to-day management) → work stream owners.
  3. Committed leadership: the number-one success factor; leaders change first.
  4. Informed stakeholders: "you cannot over-communicate." Communication continuum: awareness → understanding → acceptance → commitment.
  5. Aligned workforce: workforce planning, training, and rewards aligned with the change.

Change curve (8 stages): denial → frustration and anger → negotiation and bargaining → depression → acceptance → experimentation → discovery and delight → integration.

Three phases (Lewin): Unfreeze (analyze, show need for change) → Move (act, follow up) → Freeze (new rules, celebrate, evaluate).

Guide Questions, Straight Answers
Two questions per module, then themes that cut across modules
Module 1
Q1

Why does a low domestic value-added ratio not mean a country gains little from GVCs?

Athukorala's "volume factor" argument: in production sharing, each country does only one slice, so the local share of each product's value is naturally small. What matters is the total amount produced. A country that assembles a huge volume at 10% local value can earn more than one that makes a small volume at 40%. The Philippines' problem was not the low ratio but a dualistic incentive structure that kept export manufacturing inside enclave EPZs and stopped volume from growing across the economy.

Q2

Compare Sydow et al. and Phillips et al. on responding to COVID-19 supply shocks.

Both agree that long, lean chains proved fragile and that firms need resilience. Sydow, writing from Germany, stresses slack, redundancy, dual sourcing, back-shoring of critical items, and closer collaboration, and rejects de-globalization in favor of "intelligent coupling." Phillips focuses on reconfiguring the chain itself through redistributed manufacturing, mapping four models on risk and urgency and preferring the Local Customizer long term, but admitting barriers such as inertia and IP control. In short, Sydow adjusts the existing global network; Phillips adds local, flexible production alongside it.

Module 2
Q1

Compare FDI and licensing as channels for a Philippine firm that wants to build its own capability.

FDI is internalized: the technology stays inside the foreign firm's subsidiary, so the local firm learns only through spillovers (supplying it, copying it, or hiring its ex-staff). It suits new, complex, tacit technology. Licensing is externalized: the Philippine firm gets rights and, with a technical licensing agreement, assistance, so it controls its own learning. But licensed technology tends to be older (about 13 years vs 6 to 10 for affiliates) and can carry restrictive clauses. For capability-building, licensing plus strong in-house effort is the more direct route; FDI helps mainly through supplier linkages.

Q2

What is Alfaro's complementarities argument?

FDI benefits are not automatic. They appear only if the host has the right local conditions: enough human capital, developed financial markets, good institutions, and an outward-oriented trade policy. Without these, local firms cannot absorb the technology, and FDI may act as an enclave or steal market share. So rather than offering incentives, governments should remove barriers to linkages and improve local firms' access to skills, inputs, and finance.

Module 3
Q1

Why does WIPO say licensing is not necessarily technology transfer?

A license only grants permission to use IP. Transfer happens when the licensee actually learns to use, adapt, and improve the technology. A licensee can hold rights for years and still depend fully on the licensor. To make transfer happen, the licensee should negotiate for know-how, documentation, training, technical support, and access to improvements, and invest in its own engineers.

Q2

Explain double marginalization under push and delegation, and why an OEM might still delegate.

Under delegation, the CM buys components from the supplier and resells them inside its price. Each firm adds a markup, so the OEM's total price rises; under push, the OEM then orders less, which also hurts the CM. That is why the OEM and even the CM prefer control under push. An OEM might still delegate because the model ignores the administrative cost of managing suppliers. US automakers delegated to cut procurement staff, though cheaper e-procurement later made control attractive again (Motorola).

Module 4
Q1

Trace how a DOST institute moves from invention disclosure to signing a license.

Researchers do a prior art search and file an Invention Disclosure Form; the TLO assigns a case number and the researchers sign a Deed of Assignment to the RDI. The RDI discloses confidentially to the GFA within 30 days and files with IPOPHL within another 30 days (or within 3 months of any public disclosure). Once a Technical Review Committee confirms readiness, the TLO markets it. An adoptor sends a Letter of Intent, both sign an NDA and negotiate, the RDI does a Technology Needs Assessment, the TLO drafts the agreement, a Fairness Opinion Report is obtained (with valuation and due diligence reports), and the license is signed after the DOST Secretary's recommendation. Revenue is split 60% RDI, 40% researchers.

Q2

Why does positional bargaining fail, and how does principled negotiation fix it?

In positional bargaining, each side defends a number and treats concessions as losses, so talks stall or end in a deal that serves no one well. The US-USSR test-ban talks broke down over 3 vs 10 inspections, though neither side had discussed what inspections were for. Principled negotiation separates people from the problem, looks at the interests behind positions, creates options for mutual gain, and uses objective criteria. A strong BATNA gives power without needing a rigid bottom line.

Module 5
Q1

Why was external technology acquisition insignificant in Tajudeen et al., and what does it mean for Philippine SMEs?

Malaysian firms are mostly SMEs with tight budgets. Buying outside technology is costly and can weaken in-house skills, so it did not raise innovation. Selling or licensing technology out, scouting through social media, and a clear digital vision did. For Philippine SMEs, which face similar limits, the lesson is to build absorptive capacity first, use low-cost scouting, and treat acquisition as a complement to in-house work rather than a replacement.

Q2

Describe the reverse engineering process and why tolerance stacking threatens it.

The part is scanned by contact (CMM), non-contact (laser, structured light, CT), or hybrid methods to get a point cloud. The data is cleaned, merged, and triangulated into a mesh, segmented, and fitted with surfaces to build a CAD model, which feeds CAM, analysis, or 3D printing. Each stage adds error: the original part's manufacturing error, the scanning and modeling error, and the printing error. Added together, they can push the copy outside the design tolerance even when each step looks acceptable.

Module 6
Q1

Distinguish a business need, a business case, and a project charter.

The business need states the change the business requires, with no solution named. The business case justifies acting on it, showing benefits, costs, and risks, and secures resources. The project charter defines the project that will deliver it: objective, impact, success measures, scope, risks, time, and approvals. All three must be approved before research begins so the team solves the right problem, has funding, and is protected from second-guessing.

Q2

Distinguish technology risk from technical risk, and explain why TRLs alone are not enough for a COTS purchase.

Technology risk is the chance that a key technology will not mature in time, measured by TRL. Technical risk is the chance that the parts will not integrate into a working system, or the system into its environment, measured by SRL. A commercial off-the-shelf item may have every component at TRL 9, but if it has never been integrated into the buyer's systems, its SRL is low and it still carries integration risk.

Module 7
Q1

Explain Mortara's three evaluation factors and how the checklist scores are used.

The factors are the firm's ability to absorb the technology, compatibility with the partner, and suitability of the technology. Each checklist item gets a weight (0 to 10) and a score (-3 to +3); weight times score gives the item's points, summed into a total and compared with Box E (total weights times 3). Partner and technology totals are plotted together to show the zone (positive match, good technology with weak partner, and so on). Even with a positive total, every negative item needs a contingency plan, because the real purpose is to find concerns.

Q2

Critically compare RA 9184 and RA 12009.

Both keep competitive bidding as the default. RA 12009 moves from a rule-based system with "alternative methods" to a fit-for-purpose one with 11 modes, adding competitive dialogue, unsolicited offers, direct acquisition, direct sales, and direct procurement for STI. It adds MEARB so quality can win (price weighted 15% to 40%), expands transparency (livestreaming, beneficial ownership, e-protests), shortens timelines (60 days to award), and professionalizes the BAC. These respond to Navarro's findings on poor planning and quality, and to Jones's concerns on collusion. But Jones's root causes, informal influence and elite capture, are cultural, and a new law cannot remove them alone. RA 12009 also replaces RA 9184's penal clause with administrative liability "without prejudice to" penal laws.

Module 8
Q1

How did L Corporation turn a purchased system into its own capability?

It built all three elements of capability. Embodied: the system itself, integrated with existing infrastructure. Non-embodied: a Knowledge Transfer agreement with hands-on immersion, classroom hours written into the contract, and full documentation, plus internal staff joining installation and testing. Organizational integration: process owners shaped the design and signed off the BRD and UAT, a handholding period followed go-live, and later roll-outs were done by its own staff.

Q2

Outline a change management plan for a Philippine hospital rolling out an electronic medical record system.

Before choosing, use Mlekus's instrument with doctors, nurses, and staff to compare options on human, technology, and organization criteria, which also builds acceptance. Then follow Queensland's factors: a clear vision and case for change; governance with a steering committee, a change sponsor (e.g., the medical director), and a change agent; committed leaders who use the system first; constant two-way communication moving staff from awareness to commitment; and training and rewards aligned with the change. Apply Jones's principles by starting at the top, involving every layer, creating ownership, and speaking to each individual about what changes for them.
